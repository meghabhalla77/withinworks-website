# Reinvention Isn't a Crisis, It's a Threshold

There's a particular way senior women describe reinvention to me when we first sit down together. They call it a crisis. A crisis of confidence, a crisis of identity, a crisis of direction. Even when the actual circumstances are neutral, or even positive, a return to work after time away, a shift from a corporate career to building something of their own, a major life change that's reshaping who they are, the language that shows up is crisis language.

I understand why. Reinvention often feels destabilizing before it feels clarifying. But I want to offer a different frame, because I think the word we use for this stage shapes how we move through it. This isn't a crisis. It's a threshold.

A crisis is something that happens to you, something to survive and get past as quickly as possible. A threshold is something you walk through, deliberately, on your way to somewhere. The difference matters enormously in how you show up for the next chapter.

I work with a lot of senior women across the GCC who are standing at exactly this kind of threshold. Some are re-entering the workforce after stepping away, for children, for caregiving, for reasons that were entirely their own. Some are moving from a long corporate career into founding something, trading the structure and status of a title for the uncertainty and freedom of building alone. Some aren't changing careers at all, but are in the middle of a major life transition, a divorce, a relocation, an empty nest, that's quietly reshaping their sense of who they are, whether or not their job title has moved an inch.

What all of these have in common is that the old identity has loosened its grip before the new one has fully formed. That in-between space is uncomfortable. It's also where the real work happens, and it's not a space to rush through.

Here's something I notice constantly with clients at this threshold: they arrive wanting a plan. A new title, a new business name, a clear five-year outline. And I understand the impulse; certainty feels safer than the alternative. But when we move too quickly to the plan, we often just rebuild the old identity with a new label on it. The re-entering executive takes the first role that looks familiar, and six months later feels the same restlessness that made her question things in the first place. The corporate leader launches a business run on the same self-worth-through-output patterns that exhausted her in her last role, just with her own name on the door now.

The threshold deserves more than that. Before we build the plan, we do the work of actually meeting the person who's standing here now, not the one who existed before the break, before the transition, before the life changed. What do you actually want, separate from what you think you should want at this stage, at this age, in this market? What parts of the old identity are worth carrying forward, because they're genuinely you, and what parts were you carrying out of habit or other people's expectations?

This is slower work than most people expect coaching to be, and I think that's exactly why it works. A plan built on a half-examined identity tends to need rebuilding within a year or two. A plan built after someone has actually reckoned with who they are now tends to hold, because it's not fighting against an internal picture of themselves that no longer fits.

If you're standing at this kind of threshold right now, re-entering, reinventing, simply becoming someone slightly different than who you were two years ago, I want you to hear this clearly: there is nothing wrong with you for finding it disorienting. This isn't a problem to be fixed quickly. It's a genuine passage, and it deserves the time and depth it's asking for. What waits on the other side isn't just a new job or a new chapter. It's a version of yourself you actually recognize and trust, which is worth far more than a fast answer to a question you haven't fully asked yet.
