FAQ

A few questions people ask

Is executive coaching the same as therapy?

No, though the two can sit comfortably alongside each other. Therapy often looks back, to understand and heal. Coaching looks forward: we work with where you are now and who you're becoming next, in your leadership and in your life.

How long are sessions, and how often do we meet?

Sessions typically run 50 to 60 minutes. Within the Six-Session Journey, we usually meet every two to three weeks, which gives you room to actually live the work between conversations.

Do we meet virtually or in person?

Almost all of my coaching happens virtually, which suits the range of time zones and cities my clients work from. In-person sessions can sometimes be arranged by request.

How do I know if I'm ready for coaching?

If you're asking the question, you're probably closer than you think. Readiness tends to look like a quiet sense that something needs to shift, alongside a willingness to sit with that honestly. A discovery call is a low-pressure way to find out.

Transformation

Why Transformation Starts From the Inside Out

People often come wanting to change something external. Almost every time, we end up at the lens through which they are looking at the goal, not the goal itself.

People often come to me wanting to change something external. A team that isn’t performing. A title they haven’t been given yet. A career that no longer fits the person living it. And almost every time, within the first few sessions, we end up somewhere else entirely: not at the goal, but at the lens through which they’re looking at the goal.

This isn’t a detour. It’s the work.

I’ve spent over 25 years in rooms where people were trying to force change from the outside in. New structures, new strategies, new targets. Some of it worked, for a while. But the leaders and professionals who actually transformed, who came out the other side of a merger or a career break or a promotion genuinely different, weren’t the ones who pushed hardest against their circumstances. They were the ones who got curious about themselves first.

Here’s the belief at the center of my work: people don’t experience reality directly. They experience reality through identity, memory, and perspective. Two people can sit in the same restructuring meeting and walk out with entirely different internal experiences: one energized by the challenge, the other quietly convinced they’re about to be found out. The meeting was identical. The lens wasn’t.

This is why so much external change doesn’t stick. You can redesign an org chart, but if the person leading it still sees themselves through an old story (not strategic enough, too soft, too late to the table), the redesign will eventually bend back toward that story. You can leave a job, take the sabbatical, start the business, but if the underlying identity hasn’t shifted, the new chapter tends to rhyme uncomfortably with the old one.

I want to be clear about what I’m not saying. I’m not saying circumstances don’t matter, or that mindset alone solves structural problems. Growth, restructuring, re-entry after time away: these are real, concrete situations with real stakes. What I’m saying is that how a person meets those situations, what they believe is possible, what they think they’re allowed to want, is shaped by something deeper than the situation itself.

This is where the “inside out” language comes from, and I want to be precise about what it means, because it’s easy to hear it as something soft or vague. It isn’t about fixing what’s wrong with you. It isn’t about forcing positivity or chasing a better version of yourself through sheer will. It’s about noticing the internal lens you’ve been using, often without realizing you had a choice in the matter, and getting curious about whether it’s still serving you.

In practice, this looks like slowing down enough to ask better questions. Not “what should I do next” but “what am I believing right now that’s shaping what I think I should do next.” Not “how do I get people to follow me” but “what version of myself shows up when I feel unsure of my authority, and where did that version come from.” These aren’t therapy questions. They’re leadership and life questions, and in my experience, they’re the ones that actually move something.

I saw this firsthand building the people function at Traveazy from the ground up, through a pandemic and two mergers, growing the organization past 350 people. The technical work was demanding. But what determined whether people thrived through that much change wasn’t the org design or the process documentation. It was whether they had done the internal work to meet uncertainty without collapsing into old patterns of fear or control.

I don’t give people a formula for this, because there isn’t one that holds up across different lives. What I do is create the space for someone to actually look at their own lens, often for the first time in years, and decide consciously what they want to keep and what they’re ready to put down. The external change, the new role, the rebuilt confidence, the clearer boundaries, tends to follow. Not because it was forced, but because it finally has somewhere real to stand.

That’s the whole premise of this work. Not forcing external change. Not chasing goals for their own sake. Not fixing what’s “wrong” with you, because I don’t believe there’s something broken that needs fixing. Just changing the internal lens through which you experience your own life, and trusting that everything else has a way of reorganizing itself around that shift.

Listening

The Art of Listening: What Leaders Rarely Do

The leaders who navigated enormous change were not the ones with the best communication skills. They were the ones who heard what was not being said.

Most of the executives I work with are excellent talkers. They can walk into a board meeting and land a message in ninety seconds. They can read a room, adjust their pitch, hold a stage. What’s rarer, and what I find myself coming back to again and again in our sessions, is the ability to actually listen, to someone else, and just as importantly, to themselves.

I don’t mean listening as in waiting for your turn to speak, or listening as in nodding while your mind is already three steps ahead planning your response. I mean something slower and less automatic. The kind of listening that asks: what is this person actually saying, underneath the words they chose. What are they not saying. What would I hear if I weren’t so busy formulating what I think next.

This kind of listening is hard to practice at pace, and pace is the water most leaders swim in. Founders and senior executives are trained, often for good reason, to move fast, decide fast, respond fast. Speed becomes a proxy for competence. The person who answers quickly seems sharp. The person who pauses seems uncertain. So a habit forms, quietly, over years: process information quickly enough to respond, rather than slowly enough to actually understand. It works, until it doesn’t. Until a team member stops raising concerns because they’ve learned nobody really hears them. Until a founder misreads a co-founder’s hesitation as disengagement, when it was actually fear. Until a leader, so fluent at reading a room, loses the ability to read their own.

Here’s where this connects to something I believe at the center of my work. People don’t experience reality directly. They experience reality through identity, memory, and perspective. This is true of the person speaking to you, and it’s true of you as the one listening. When someone tells you their team is struggling with a deadline, you’re not just hearing information about a deadline. You’re hearing it filtered through your own history with deadlines, your own relationship to pressure, your own story about what it means when someone says they’re struggling. Real listening means noticing that filter is there, and trying to hear past it, to what the other person’s own identity and memory and perspective are actually shaping in what they say.

I saw the cost of not doing this up close, building the people function at Traveazy through a period of enormous change, a pandemic and two mergers layered on top of ordinary growth. In that kind of environment, everyone is talking, constantly, and very little of it is actually being heard. People say “I’m fine” in a tone that isn’t fine. They say “no concerns” while their body language says otherwise. The leaders who navigated that period well weren’t the ones with the best communication skills in the traditional sense. They were the ones who had trained themselves to hear what wasn’t being said directly, and to ask one more question instead of accepting the first answer.

This is also, not coincidentally, the core discipline of coaching. When I sit with a client, my job in that hour isn’t to have the smartest response ready. It’s to listen generatively, which means listening in a way that creates space for the other person to hear themselves more clearly, often for the first time. I’m not analyzing what they say against a mental checklist. I’m noticing the words they return to, the ones they stumble over, the moment their voice changes when they get close to something true. That kind of attention is a practice, not a personality trait. Nobody is naturally gifted at it forever. It has to be tended.

The same applies to listening inward, which is where I’d ask any leader reading this to start. Most people are far better at listening to their teams, their partners, their clients, than they are at listening to themselves. They override the quiet discomfort that’s trying to tell them something isn’t sustainable. They talk over the part of themselves that already knows the answer to the hard decision, because the answer is inconvenient. Slowing down enough to actually listen to your own thinking, without immediately managing or fixing or arguing with it, is one of the most underused leadership skills I know.

None of this requires more time than most leaders think. It requires a different kind of attention in the time you already have. A pause before you respond. A second question instead of a first assumption. A willingness to sit with what someone said, or what you just noticed in yourself, before rushing to decide what it means. In a world moving as fast as this one, that kind of listening isn’t a soft skill. It’s a strategic one, and in my experience, it’s the one that changes everything else.

Leadership

The Saboteurs Sitting at Your Leadership Table

Every leader has an internal cast of characters that shows up under pressure. Most have been running on their fuel so long they have mistaken them for strengths.

There’s a particular moment in coaching that I’ve come to recognize almost immediately. A leader is describing a decision they made, or didn’t make, and partway through the story, their voice changes. It gets a little faster, a little more certain, a little more rehearsed. That’s usually the moment a saboteur has taken over the story.

Saboteurs are the automatic patterns of thought that protected you at some point in your life, often early on, and that now run quietly in the background of your leadership without your permission. They’re not character flaws. They’re not signs of weakness. They’re old strategies that made sense once and have simply overstayed their welcome.

I want to make this concrete, because it’s easy for “saboteurs” to sound like an abstract coaching term rather than something you can actually spot in your own week.

Take the Controller. This is the leader who has a hard time delegating, not because they don’t trust their team’s competence, but because some part of them equates control with safety. In a growth phase, this shows up as a founder who still reviews every deck, every hire, every client email, long after the team has outgrown that level of oversight. It’s exhausting for them and, eventually, disempowering for everyone around them. The irony is that this pattern often gets someone promoted in the first place. Being hands-on, thorough, unwilling to let things slip, that’s rewarded early in a career. It just doesn’t scale.

Or take the Pleaser, which shows up constantly in senior women I work with, especially at points of professional reinvention. The Pleaser leader has built real skill at reading a room and meeting people’s needs, which is genuinely valuable. But under pressure, it can tip into over-functioning: saying yes to scope that isn’t theirs, softening feedback until it loses its usefulness, staying quiet in a meeting to avoid being seen as difficult. I’ve watched capable, senior people talk themselves out of asking for what they’re worth because some old part of them is still calibrated to keep everyone comfortable.

There’s the Hyper-Achiever, who ties self-worth so tightly to accomplishment that rest starts to feel like risk. This one is common among leaders who’ve climbed fast, and it’s often invisible to the person living it, because the results look like success from the outside. Nobody questions a leader who’s always delivering. But I’ve sat with people at the top of that climb who couldn’t name a single thing they wanted outside of the next milestone, because the Hyper-Achiever had quietly taken over the whole operating system.

And there’s the Restless one, always scanning for the next opportunity, the next pivot, the next version of the plan, which can look like ambition but is sometimes an inability to be fully present with what’s already working.

None of these patterns are the enemy. That’s the part people find most relieving when we name their own saboteur out loud. You don’t need to fight it or shame it into silence. It was doing a job. The Controller kept you safe when delegating meant risking your name on someone else’s mistake. The Pleaser kept you safe when disagreement felt dangerous. The work isn’t eradication. It’s recognition, and then choice.

This is part of what we do together in the Six-Session Coaching Journey, in the module on saboteurs and the inner leader. We identify which patterns are running the show, usually more than one, and we get specific about when they show up: which meetings, which relationships, which kinds of decisions trigger them most. Once a pattern has a name and a face, it loses a lot of its automatic power. You start to catch it in real time, mid-meeting, mid-email, and you get a genuine choice about whether to let the old pattern drive or to respond from somewhere steadier.

That steadier place is what I call the Inner Leader, the part of you that isn’t reactive, that can see the saboteur clearly without being run by it. It’s not a personality overhaul. It’s already in you. Most of the leaders I work with don’t need to become someone new. They need to recognize who’s been quietly steering, thank that part for its service, and take the wheel back themselves.

Transitions

What No One Tells You About Executive Transitions

The hardest part is rarely the new role itself. It is the in-between: after one identity has ended and before the next has fully arrived.

I started my career in banking, 14 years across Barclays and Citibank, before spending the rest of my career building people functions across the UAE, the wider Middle East, and Asia. Most recently, I was the founding Chief People Officer at Traveazy Group, where I built the function from scratch and helped lead the organization through growth to more than 350 employees, a global pandemic, and two mergers.

I’m not sharing that history to establish credentials for their own sake. I’m sharing it because it’s the source of everything I now believe about executive transitions, and most of what I believe didn’t come from a book. It came from being in the room, repeatedly, while people navigated the exact inflection points my clients are navigating now.

Here’s the first thing 25 years taught me: the technical part of a transition is rarely the hard part.

When Traveazy was scaling, we had frameworks for almost everything, hiring plans, org design, compensation structures, integration playbooks for the mergers. Frameworks matter. But I watched, again and again, that the leaders who struggled during that growth weren’t struggling with the frameworks. They were struggling with who they had to become to hold the next size of the company. A founder who’d built the business on instinct and speed suddenly needed to lead through structure and delegation, and that identity shift was harder for them than any org redesign.

The second thing: transitions rarely announce themselves as identity work. They arrive disguised as operational problems. “We need a better leadership team.” “This restructuring is taking too long.” “I can’t get my direct reports to own their decisions.” Underneath almost every one of those statements, when you sit with it long enough, is a leader who hasn’t yet grown into the next version of themselves that the role now requires. The org chart changed faster than the person leading it.

I saw this with painful clarity through Covid. Nobody’s playbook accounted for what we went through. What got teams through wasn’t the contingency plan, though we had one. It was whether the leaders at the top could tolerate genuine uncertainty without either freezing or overcorrecting into control. Some could. Some couldn’t, not because they lacked competence, but because their sense of themselves as leaders was built on having answers, and Covid didn’t offer many.

The mergers taught me something related. A merger is, on paper, a legal and operational event. In practice, it’s an identity event for everyone involved, for the people whose team no longer exists as it did, for the leader whose authority just got redrawn, for the culture that has to absorb another culture without losing itself entirely. The organizations that integrated well weren’t the ones with the cleanest process documents. They were the ones where leadership had done enough internal work to hold complexity and ambiguity without needing to resolve it prematurely just to feel in control again.

This is what I bring to the Scaling Leader clients I work with now: senior executives and founders across the GCC who are navigating real growth, restructuring, or a leadership transition. I’m not coming to this as an outside consultant theorizing about change management. I built a people function through the exact kind of growth and disruption many of my clients are living through right now, and I know from direct experience that the org design is the visible ten percent of the work.

The other ninety percent is quieter. It’s the leader learning to trust a team they used to do everything themselves. It’s the executive stepping into a bigger mandate who still, somewhere, feels like the person who got lucky rather than the person who earned it. It’s the founder realizing that the instincts that built the company are not the same instincts that will scale it, and grieving that a little, even while being excited about what’s next.

None of this shows up on a strategy slide. But after 25 years across banking floors, boardrooms, and a company I helped build from the ground up, I can say with real confidence that it’s the part that determines whether a transition actually succeeds or just gets survived. My work now is to help leaders do that quieter, harder part deliberately, instead of leaving it to chance the way most organizations still do.

Reinvention

Standing at the Reinvention Threshold

Reinvention rarely arrives as a clear call. It arrives as a quiet friction, a sense that the life you have built no longer fits the person you have become.

There’s a particular way senior women describe reinvention to me when we first sit down together. They call it a crisis. A crisis of confidence, a crisis of identity, a crisis of direction. Even when the actual circumstances are neutral, or even positive, a return to work after time away, a shift from a corporate career to building something of their own, a major life change that’s reshaping who they are, the language that shows up is crisis language.

I understand why. Reinvention often feels destabilizing before it feels clarifying. But I want to offer a different frame, because I think the word we use for this stage shapes how we move through it. This isn’t a crisis. It’s a threshold.

A crisis is something that happens to you, something to survive and get past as quickly as possible. A threshold is something you walk through, deliberately, on your way to somewhere. The difference matters enormously in how you show up for the next chapter.

I work with a lot of senior women across the GCC who are standing at exactly this kind of threshold. Some are re-entering the workforce after stepping away, for children, for caregiving, for reasons that were entirely their own. Some are moving from a long corporate career into founding something, trading the structure and status of a title for the uncertainty and freedom of building alone. Some aren’t changing careers at all, but are in the middle of a major life transition, a divorce, a relocation, an empty nest, that’s quietly reshaping their sense of who they are, whether or not their job title has moved an inch.

What all of these have in common is that the old identity has loosened its grip before the new one has fully formed. That in-between space is uncomfortable. It’s also where the real work happens, and it’s not a space to rush through.

Here’s something I notice constantly with clients at this threshold: they arrive wanting a plan. A new title, a new business name, a clear five-year outline. And I understand the impulse; certainty feels safer than the alternative. But when we move too quickly to the plan, we often just rebuild the old identity with a new label on it. The re-entering executive takes the first role that looks familiar, and six months later feels the same restlessness that made her question things in the first place. The corporate leader launches a business run on the same self-worth-through-output patterns that exhausted her in her last role, just with her own name on the door now.

The threshold deserves more than that. Before we build the plan, we do the work of actually meeting the person who’s standing here now, not the one who existed before the break, before the transition, before the life changed. What do you actually want, separate from what you think you should want at this stage, at this age, in this market? What parts of the old identity are worth carrying forward, because they’re genuinely you, and what parts were you carrying out of habit or other people’s expectations?

This is slower work than most people expect coaching to be, and I think that’s exactly why it works. A plan built on a half-examined identity tends to need rebuilding within a year or two. A plan built after someone has actually reckoned with who they are now tends to hold, because it’s not fighting against an internal picture of themselves that no longer fits.

If you’re standing at this kind of threshold right now, re-entering, reinventing, simply becoming someone slightly different than who you were two years ago, I want you to hear this clearly: there is nothing wrong with you for finding it disorienting. This isn’t a problem to be fixed quickly. It’s a genuine passage, and it deserves the time and depth it’s asking for. What waits on the other side isn’t just a new job or a new chapter. It’s a version of yourself you actually recognize and trust, which is worth far more than a fast answer to a question you haven’t fully asked yet.

Coaching

Coaching Is Not Advice, And That Is the Point

The most common misconception is that a good coach tells you what to do. A good coach does something harder: they help you hear what you already know.

People are sometimes surprised, in our first session together, that I don’t tell them what to do. I understand the surprise. After 25 years in banking and building people functions through significant organizational change, I’ve formed plenty of opinions along the way, and clients often arrive expecting me to hand some of them over. Tell me what to do about this founder relationship. Tell me whether to take the promotion. Tell me if I should leave.

I won’t, and I want to explain why, because it’s not modesty and it’s not a technique for its own sake. It’s the actual foundation of how I believe change happens.

I don’t give advice. I create a space for people to tap into their own wisdom and potential. That’s not a soft sentiment I include for warmth. It’s a working principle, and it comes from a genuine belief that my clients are not broken, not lacking, not waiting for someone smarter to hand them the answer. They are resourceful, capable, and whole, even in the moments they feel furthest from that. Especially in those moments, actually.

Here’s the practical problem with advice, beyond the philosophical one. When I tell you what to do, I’m working from my history, my values, my risk tolerance, my read of your situation filtered through my own experience. Even when the advice sounds right, even when you follow it and it goes well, you haven’t built the internal muscle of trusting your own judgment. You’ve just outsourced the decision to someone whose experience isn’t actually the one you have to live. And the next hard decision arrives, and you need someone else to hand you an answer again.

Coaching works differently. When I ask you the harder question instead of giving you the easier answer, something shifts that’s more durable than any single decision. You start to hear your own thinking more clearly. You notice which of your instincts you trust and which ones you’ve learned to doubt, often for reasons that have nothing to do with whether they’re actually reliable. Over six sessions, or over years, that muscle gets stronger, and it doesn’t disappear when our work together ends. That’s the whole point. I’m not trying to become someone you need indefinitely. I’m trying to become someone you eventually don’t.

I should be honest about what this requires of me, because it’s not the easier path for the coach either. It would often be faster to just tell a client what I think. I have opinions, formed over a long career, and there are sessions where I can see the shape of what I’d do in their position. Holding that back, and instead asking the question that lets them find their own shape of the answer, takes real discipline. It means sitting with silence longer than feels comfortable. It means resisting the pull to rescue someone from the discomfort of not yet knowing.

But I’ve watched what happens when clients arrive at their own answers instead of borrowing mine. The decision lands differently. It’s not just correct on paper, it’s theirs, which means they can actually stand behind it when it gets hard, and it will get hard. A decision handed down as advice tends to wobble the first time reality pushes back on it. A decision someone found through their own clarity tends to hold, because it was never separate from who they are in the first place.

This is also why I don’t believe in one-size-fits-all frameworks applied the same way to every client. The questions I ask a scaling founder wrestling with delegation are different from the questions I ask a senior woman rebuilding her sense of self after a career break, not because one deserves more rigor than the other, but because the wisdom I’m helping each of them access is entirely their own, shaped by their own history and context.

So if you come to our work together wanting me to hand you an answer, I’ll disappoint you, gently and on purpose. What I’ll offer instead is something I’ve come to trust more than any advice I could give: a space steady enough, and honest enough, for you to hear what you already know.

Gratitude

The Power of Gratitude

Gratitude is often reduced to a journaling habit. Used well, it is a second lens, one that lets a person register what is working without losing the instinct that built their success in the first place.

I want to be careful with this one, because gratitude has become one of those words that gets used so often it stops meaning much. Journal three things you’re grateful for. Post a sunset with a caption about blessings. I understand why the advice exists, and I don’t think it’s wrong exactly, but in my work with executives and founders under real pressure, and with women rebuilding their sense of self at a career inflection point, that version of gratitude tends to land as one more task on an already full list. Something to feel guilty about not doing rather than something that actually changes how a person moves through their life.

So let me describe what I actually mean, because it’s a different thing entirely, and it’s something I return to often in coaching, not as a technique but as a lens.

The leaders I work with are, almost without exception, oriented toward what’s missing. This isn’t a flaw. It’s often exactly what made them capable of building something significant. The founder who scans for the gap in the strategy before anyone else sees it. The executive who notices the one metric that’s off in a quarter full of good ones. That instinct is valuable, and I would never coach someone out of it entirely. But left unchecked, it becomes the only lens available, and a person can build an entire career, even a successful one, standing on a foundation that only ever registers as insufficient.

Gratitude, in the way I mean it, isn’t the opposite of that instinct. It’s a second lens, held alongside the first, not replacing it. It’s the practice of actually letting yourself register what’s working, what’s already been built, who has already shown up for you, before moving straight to the next gap. Not because the gap doesn’t matter. Because a person who can only see the gap eventually can’t see anything else, and that’s not sustainable, no matter how driven you are.

I think about this often in the context of the women I coach who are at a threshold, leaving a role, re-entering after a break, redefining what success means to them after years of chasing someone else’s definition. There’s a particular kind of gratitude that matters here, and it’s not gratitude for the circumstances. It’s gratitude for the self who got them through the circumstances. I ask clients, sometimes, to actually sit with everything a younger version of themselves navigated to get to this exact point, the decisions made with incomplete information, the resilience nobody applauded because it looked like just getting through a Tuesday. Most people have never once thanked themselves for that. They’ve moved straight from one demand to the next without ever turning around to notice what they survived, and built, along the way.

This connects to something I believe about how people experience their own lives. People don’t experience reality directly. They experience it through identity, memory, and perspective. A person who has trained their perspective to notice only deficit will experience an objectively full life as somehow lacking. A person who has trained themselves to notice, honestly and specifically, what is actually working, experiences the same objective circumstances quite differently, not because reality changed but because the lens did. Gratitude, done properly, is lens work. It’s not about pretending things are fine. It’s about seeing the whole picture instead of only the part your habitual attention has trained you to see.

I felt this myself building the people function at Traveazy through years that included a pandemic and two mergers. There was no shortage of things to worry about, genuinely, urgently. But I also learned, out of necessity, to notice what was holding. The team members who stayed steady. The systems that, imperfectly, worked. My own capacity to keep functioning under pressure I hadn’t been tested by before. Noticing that wasn’t naïve optimism. It was accurate, and it was fuel, in a way that constant vigilance for what might go wrong never could be on its own.

If you want a practical version of this, it isn’t a journal, unless a journal genuinely works for you. It’s a habit of pausing, at the end of a hard day or a hard quarter, and asking a more specific question than “what am I grateful for.” Ask instead: what actually held, here, that I haven’t given myself credit for. Who showed up. What did I do that I’d have doubted myself capable of a year ago. Answer specifically, not generically. That specificity is what separates real gratitude from the platitude it’s often mistaken for, and it’s the difference between a practice that changes how you lead and one that just makes for a nice photo.
